How to make a proper Kiwi thickshake

By The Wooden Spoon team · 11 November 2026 · 7 min read

A proper Kiwi thickshake is just good ice cream blended with a splash of cold milk until it is so thick the straw stands up on its own. For one big glass, use about three generous scoops of ice cream (roughly 250 g) and 100–125 ml of cold full-cream milk, blend in short bursts for 20–30 seconds, and pour into a glass you have chilled in the freezer. More ice cream makes it thicker; more milk makes it easier to drink. That is the whole secret, and the rest of this guide is about getting the details right.

What makes a thickshake different from a milkshake?

A thickshake contains ice cream; a classic New Zealand milkshake often doesn't. Order a "milkshake" at an old-school takeaway and you may get milk whipped with flavoured syrup, served frothy in a tall cup. Ask for a thickshake and you get the ice cream version: colder, creamier and slower to drink.

The naming isn't just a Kiwi quirk. Wikipedia's milkshake article notes that the words for ice cream shakes and other flavoured milks vary from place to place, and that an ice cream milkshake may be called a thick shake to tell the two apart.

The same article says milkshakes started in the United States around the turn of the 20th century, and that ice cream became a common addition in the early 1900s. It also credits a Walgreens employee with adding two scoops of vanilla ice cream to a malted milk drink in 1922, which helped make the malted shake famous.

The basic thickshake recipe

Ice cream, milk, a cold glass and a blender. That is all you need for one large thickshake or two smaller ones.

Ingredients

  • 3 generous scoops of ice cream, about 250 g
  • 100–125 ml cold full-cream milk
  • Optional: 1–2 tablespoons of sauce or syrup, or a handful of mix-ins

Method

  1. Chill the glass. Put it in the freezer for 10–15 minutes while you get organised.
  2. Soften the ice cream slightly. Leave the tub on the bench for about five minutes. Rock-hard scoops strain the blender and need more milk to get moving. Our guide to softening ice cream explains how to do this without melting the edges.
  3. Milk in first. Pour the milk into the blender jug, then add the scoops. The liquid at the bottom helps the blades catch.
  4. Blend in short bursts. Pulse for a few seconds at a time, 20–30 seconds in total. Stop as soon as there are no lumps.
  5. Check the texture. Too thick to move? Add a tablespoon of milk and pulse once. Too thin? Add another scoop.
  6. Pour and serve straight away. Give it a wide straw and a long spoon.

How to get the texture right

Thickness comes down to the ratio of ice cream to milk and how cold everything stays. Blend too long and the motor warms the mix, which thins it fast.

Scoop sizes vary, so treat these as starting points and adjust by eye. A few more tips:

  • Full-cream milk gives a richer, smoother shake. Trim milk works but tastes thinner.
  • No ice cubes. Ice waters the shake down and leaves crunchy shards. Let the ice cream do the chilling.
  • No blender? Soften the ice cream a little more, then mash it with the milk in a jug using a whisk, or shake it hard in a sealed jar.

Flavour ideas for summer

Start with a flavour you would happily eat from the tub, because the shake will taste like that flavour, only colder. Here are a few combinations worth trying.

  • Vanilla and malt. Add a heaped tablespoon of malted milk powder to a vanilla shake for that old-fashioned milk bar taste.
  • Hokey pokey. A Kiwi favourite. Blend vanilla or caramel ice cream, then fold in crushed hokey pokey at the end so it stays crunchy. Our hokey pokey guide covers making the honeycomb itself.
  • Coffee. Add a cooled shot of espresso to a vanilla or coffee shake. Fans of our Vietnamese Coffee flavour will know where we are going with this. If you would rather have the coffee hot, make an affogato instead.
  • Double chocolate. Chocolate ice cream, a spoonful of cocoa or chocolate sauce, and grated dark chocolate on top. Our hot fudge sauce works well swirled around the inside of the glass (let it cool first so it doesn't melt the shake).
  • Summer berries. Blend a handful of strawberries or frozen raspberries with vanilla ice cream. Frozen fruit keeps the shake extra cold.

Chunky ice creams with cookie dough, brownie or biscuit pieces make brilliant shakes. Blend for just a few seconds so you keep some of the pieces whole. Browse our flavours for ideas.

Making a dairy-free thickshake

Swap in a dairy-free ice cream and a plant milk, and the method stays exactly the same. Oat milk is a good match because it is creamy and mild.

Our dairy-free range is made with an oat milk base, so a scoop or three makes a rich shake. Plant milks vary in thickness, so start with a little less than you would use with dairy milk and add more as needed. Our post on dairy-free ice cream explains how the different bases behave.

Allergy and safety notes

Read every label, including the extras. A thickshake can pick up allergens from the ice cream, the milk, the sauce and the toppings.

  • Milk is in almost every regular thickshake. Use dairy-free ice cream and plant milk if someone can't have it, and clean the blender jug thoroughly between batches.
  • Malted milk powder is made with malted barley and wheat flour, according to Wikipedia's milkshake article, so it isn't suitable for anyone avoiding gluten.
  • Nuts, peanuts, eggs and soy can turn up in mix-ins, sauces and biscuits. Our ice cream allergens guide and the ingredients and allergens list are good places to check.
  • Raw eggs. Some old recipes add a raw egg for richness. We'd skip it; good ice cream already brings plenty of body.

Serving thickshakes to a crowd

Prep the glasses and toppings first, then blend to order. They thin quickly, so don't make them ahead.

  • Chill the glasses on a tray in the freezer before guests arrive.
  • Set out toppings in small bowls: crushed hokey pokey, sprinkles, chopped chocolate, whipped cream.
  • Offer smaller glasses for kids. Big gulps through a straw are a classic cause of brain freeze.

Hosting a bigger party? Our guide to how much ice cream to buy for a party helps with quantities, and an ice cream sundae bar pairs nicely with a shake station.

Quick thickshake checklist

  • Glass chilled in the freezer.
  • Ice cream softened for about five minutes.
  • Roughly two parts ice cream to one part cold milk.
  • Milk in the jug first, then the scoops.
  • Short bursts, stop as soon as it is smooth.
  • Labels checked for allergens.

That's it: a few scoops of ice cream, a splash of milk and half a minute with the blender, and you have the best drink of the summer. For more ideas, our FAQs cover the basics, and for the latest on where to find our tubs this season, follow us on Instagram and Facebook.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a milkshake and a thickshake?

In New Zealand a milkshake is usually milk whizzed with flavoured syrup, light and easy to drink through a straw. A thickshake adds ice cream, so it comes out heavier, colder and creamier. Wikipedia notes that an ice cream milkshake may be called a thick shake to tell it apart from other flavoured milk drinks.

How do I make a thickshake thicker?

Use more ice cream and less milk, and keep everything cold. Start with roughly two parts ice cream to one part milk, blend in short bursts and stop as soon as it is smooth. Chill the glass in the freezer first. Over-blending warms the ice cream and thins the shake, so stop early.

Can I make a thickshake without a blender?

Yes. Let the ice cream soften for a few minutes, then mash it with the milk in a big jug using a whisk or fork, or shake it hard in a well-sealed jar. It won't be as smooth or frothy as a blended one, but it will still be thick, cold and very good.

Can I make a dairy-free thickshake?

Yes. Use a dairy-free ice cream and a plant milk such as oat milk, which blends smoothly and has a mild flavour. Check every label, including sauces and toppings, for milk and other allergens, and wash the blender well if someone in the house has a milk allergy.
