What makes small-batch ice cream different?

By The Wooden Spoon team · 12 October 2026 · 5 min read

Small-batch ice cream is made in small quantities, often by hand, with more control over every step: how the base is made, how much air goes in, and what the mix-ins are. Compared with mass-produced ice cream, it tends to be denser, richer and more flavourful, with fewer shortcuts. You can usually tell the difference by the ingredient list, the weight of the tub and how the ice cream melts.

Small-batch vs mass-made at a glance

Small-batchMass-made
BaseOften cooked from scratchUsually a pre-made mix
AirUsually less, so denserOften more, so lighter
Mix-insOften made in-houseUsually bought in
FlavoursSeasonal, experimental, limited runsFixed, long-running range
ConsistencySmall variations between batchesIdentical every time

The base: cooked vs ready-made

The base is the foundation of every flavour. Many large producers start with a ready-made liquid or powdered mix that goes straight into the machine. Many small producers cook their own base, heating milk, cream and sugar into a custard-style mix, then cooling and aging it. Cooking and aging give the ingredients time to bind, which helps the finished ice cream feel smooth and creamy. It is slower and fussier, but that step is where a lot of the texture comes from.

At Wooden Spoon, every batch starts this way, on the stove, with organic New Zealand milk and cream. You can follow the full process on our how it's made page.

Air: the hidden ingredient

All ice cream contains air, because churning whips it in as the mix freezes. The amount of extra volume is called overrun. Ice cream is often sold by volume rather than weight, so more air means more tubs from the same mix, and a lighter, foamier product that melts fast. Less air gives a dense, heavy ice cream with more flavour in every spoonful.

A quick test: pick up two tubs of the same size. The heavier one almost always has less air.

Fat and solids

Milk fat carries flavour and gives ice cream its smoothness, and milk solids give it body. In New Zealand, the original trans-Tasman food standard for ice cream, Standard 2.5.6, required at least 100 g of milk fat per kilogram and at least 168 g of food solids per litre for a product to be called ice cream. Small-batch makers often go well beyond the minimum, using cream and whole milk rather than vegetable fats or extra powders.

That is also why "frozen confection" and "ice cream" are not always the same thing on a label. If the front of the pack avoids the words ice cream, have a look at the ingredients.

Mix-ins made by hand

The swirls and pieces are where small-batch ice cream really stands apart. Instead of buying in ready-made caramel, cookie dough or brittle, many craft makers make their own. We cook our own caramel for Smokey Pokey, make the hokey pokey pieces, bake pie crusts and crumbles, and cold-brew coffee for at least 24 hours for our coffee ice cream. House-made pieces taste fresher and can be designed to stay pleasant when frozen.

Flavour freedom

Small batches make experimentation possible. If a flavour flops, it is one small run, not a factory full. That is why small-batch makers can try seasonal fruit, local collaborations and slightly wild ideas. Our own limited edition archive includes everything from pandan cake and mango sticky rice to pear with blue cheese, and our Freeze Craft collaborations paired us with other New Zealand producers.

Why batches can vary a little

Small-batch ice cream is not made by a fully automated line, so you may notice small differences from one batch to the next. Fruit is the biggest variable: berries and stone fruit taste different through the season, so a raspberry ice cream can be a touch sharper in one batch than another. Hand-made swirls and pieces are spread through the tub by people rather than machines, so one scoop may have more caramel than the next.

Most ice cream lovers see that as part of the charm. What should stay the same is the quality: a creamy texture, real flavour and ingredients you can recognise. If something ever seems wrong with a tub, the maker will want to know, so tell them.

How to spot a quality tub

  1. Read the ingredients. Milk and cream near the top is a good sign; a long list of gums, colours and flavourings is not.
  2. Check the name. "Ice cream" has a legal minimum for milk fat; other names may not.
  3. Feel the weight. Heavier for the same size usually means less air.
  4. Watch the melt. Good ice cream melts slowly into a creamy pool; very airy ice cream collapses into foam.
  5. Taste the mix-ins. Real pieces taste like what they claim to be.

Looking after it once it's home

Dense, rich ice cream rewards good care. Store it at -18°C or colder at the back of the freezer, press baking paper onto the surface after opening, and let it soften for a few minutes before scooping so the flavour can come through. Our guides to storing ice cream and softening it for perfect scoops have the details. And if you want to compare styles, read gelato vs ice cream vs frozen custard.

Frequently asked questions

What is overrun in ice cream?

Overrun is the amount of air whipped into ice cream as it churns and freezes, expressed as a percentage of the original mix. A product with 100% overrun has doubled in volume, so half of it is air. Lower overrun means denser, richer ice cream that melts more slowly.

Is small-batch ice cream always better?

Not automatically. Small batches give makers more control and room to experiment, but quality still depends on ingredients and skill. The best test is simple: read the ingredient list, feel how heavy the tub is for its size, and taste how it melts. Good ice cream feels creamy, not icy or foamy.

Why is craft ice cream more expensive?

Ingredients and time. Organic milk and cream, real fruit, house-made sauces and pieces cost more than ready-made mixes and flavourings, and small batches take more hands-on work. Denser ice cream with less air also means you get more actual ice cream in every tub.
