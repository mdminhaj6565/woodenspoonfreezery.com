NZ summer fruit and ice cream pairings

By The Wooden Spoon team · 19 November 2026 · 7 min read

The best NZ summer fruit and ice cream pairings keep it simple: strawberries with vanilla or white chocolate, cherries with dark chocolate, grilled apricots and peaches with caramel or honeycomb, roasted plums with vanilla custard, and berries with almost anything creamy. Pick ripe fruit, give it one small bit of help and let the ice cream do the rest.

What fruit is in season in a New Zealand summer?

From December to February, New Zealand has berries, stone fruit and melons at their best. PAK'nSAVE's seasonal guide lists strawberries, raspberries, boysenberries, blackberries, blackcurrants, cherries, apricots, peaches, nectarines, plums, watermelon and rock melon among its summer fruit.

Stone fruit starts arriving from late spring. FreshPlaza reported in November 2024 that the summer fruit harvest began in Hawke's Bay and Marlborough, with Central Otago, the biggest growing region, following soon after.

Ripe fruit is the whole point here. A hard, under-ripe peach won't be saved by even the best scoop.

Match the fruit's character to the ice cream: sharp fruit loves rich, creamy flavours; very sweet fruit likes something a bit darker, saltier or toastier for balance.

Berries: strawberries, raspberries and boysenberries

Berries are the easiest pairing of all: wash, dry, and pile them on. The only trick worth learning is macerating, which turns plain strawberries into something that tastes like a sauce without any cooking.

  1. Hull and slice the strawberries.
  2. Sprinkle with sugar, about a teaspoon per cup of fruit, plus a squeeze of lemon or orange juice if you like.
  3. Leave for 15–30 minutes. The sugar draws out the juice and you end up with glossy berries in their own syrup.
  4. Spoon over the ice cream just before serving.

Raspberries are too delicate for much fuss; crush a few with a fork and leave the rest whole. Boysenberries and blackberries are tarter, so they shine with rich, creamy ice cream, or cooked into a compote (see below).

A note on frozen berries. Out of season, frozen berries are handy, but take care with imported ones. New Zealand Food Safety has advised briefly boiling imported frozen berries, or heating them above 85°C for one minute, before eating them, and says washing won't remove the risk. It also says elderly people and anyone with chronic liver damage should avoid frozen berries that haven't been heat treated. A compote takes care of this neatly.

Cherries: the Christmas pairing

Cherries and dark chocolate are a classic for good reason: the fruit's sweet-sharp juice cuts through the richness. Vanilla is the gentler choice.

  • Pit them first. Nobody wants to fish stones out of a sundae. A cherry pitter is quick, or halve them with a small knife and flick the stone out.
  • Fresh or roasted. Perfect cherries need nothing. Slightly tart ones are lovely roasted at 200°C for about 10 minutes with a spoonful of sugar, until they soften and release a dark syrup.
  • Little ones: always remove the stones for small children, and cut cherries into halves or quarters.

For the full chocolate treatment, add a drizzle of our homemade hot fudge sauce.

Stone fruit: apricots, peaches, nectarines and plums

Stone fruit is best warm. A few minutes on a hot barbecue or griddle caramelises the cut side, softens the flesh and makes the fruit taste even sweeter, and warm fruit next to cold ice cream is a very good contrast.

To grill: halve and stone the fruit, brush the cut side with a little neutral oil or melted butter, and place it cut side down on a hot, clean grill for 3–5 minutes, until marked and just tender. Peaches and nectarines can go in thick wedges. Rest for a couple of minutes before serving.

To roast: lay halved plums or apricots cut side up in a dish, sprinkle with a little brown sugar and roast at 200°C for 15–20 minutes, until jammy and bubbling.

Apricots are tangy, so they suit caramel and honeycomb flavours; our post on hokey pokey ice cream explains why that toffee crunch works so well against tart fruit. Peaches love plain vanilla. Plums go deep and jammy when roasted, which makes them perfect with a custardy vanilla.

Safety: sugar and fruit juice get extremely hot in the oven or pan. Let roasted fruit cool for a few minutes and keep small hands away from the dish.

A five-minute berry compote

A compote is just fruit cooked briefly with a little sugar until it turns saucy. It rescues tart berries and keeps in the fridge for a few days.

  • 2 cups berries (fresh, or frozen straight from the freezer)
  • 2–3 tablespoons sugar, to taste
  • A squeeze of lemon juice

Put everything in a small saucepan over medium heat. Bring to a steady boil, stirring, and let it bubble for 2–3 minutes until the berries burst and the juice thickens slightly. If you are using imported frozen berries, make sure the whole pot comes to a proper boil. Cool before serving; it thickens as it cools.

Want fruit inside the ice cream instead of on top? Cook the compote down until it is jammy, chill it, and swirl it through softened ice cream. Our no-churn ice cream guide shows how to ripple a sauce through without turning the whole tub icy.

Summer fruit and ice cream checklist

  • Wash berries and fruit just before using, then pat dry.
  • Pit cherries and stone fruit; quarter cherries for small children.
  • Heat imported frozen berries to a proper boil before eating.
  • Let grilled or roasted fruit cool a few minutes before it meets the ice cream.
  • Cut fruit left out on a warm table for a couple of hours is best thrown away.
  • Check for allergens in toppings, sauces and the ice cream itself.

For a crowd, set the fruit and sauces out like a sundae bar and serve in small batches. Our post on how long ice cream can sit out explains why. If anyone at the table has a food allergy, our ice cream allergens guide and ingredients and allergens list are good places to check first.

Frequently asked questions

What fruit goes best with vanilla ice cream?

Almost any ripe summer fruit, which is why vanilla is the safest bet for a fruit bowl. Strawberries, cherries, grilled peaches and roasted plums are our favourites. Vanilla lets the fruit lead, so it works when you have a mix of fruit on the table and can't decide which flavour to open.

Should fruit be warm or cold with ice cream?

Both work, for different reasons. Cold, sliced fruit keeps things fresh and light on a hot day. Warm grilled or roasted fruit melts the ice cream a little into a sauce, which is lovely after dinner. Just let cooked fruit cool for a few minutes so it doesn't turn the scoop to soup.

Do I need to cook frozen berries before serving them with ice cream?

If they are imported frozen berries, New Zealand Food Safety has advised heating them first: a brief boil, or above 85°C for one minute. Washing does not remove the risk. Turning them into a quick compote solves it, and the cooled sauce is excellent spooned over ice cream.

How do I stop fruit making my ice cream icy?

Serve the fruit alongside or on top, rather than stirring raw fruit through a tub you plan to refreeze. Raw fruit is mostly water, and water freezes into hard crystals. If you want fruit inside the ice cream, cook it down into a thick sauce first and swirl it through.
